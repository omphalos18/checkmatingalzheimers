Our Research

Why chess? Here's what we're exploring.

Chess is one of the most mentally demanding games there is. Caton and Aman are looking into whether playing it regularly might help support brain health as people age, and what that could mean for Alzheimer's awareness down the road.

In collaboration with Clemson University Ages 65+ welcome to learn more
The Idea

Chess may touch some of the same regions Alzheimer's affects.

Alzheimer's disease progressively damages the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and other brain regions responsible for memory, planning, and spatial reasoning. Playing chess draws on a lot of those same functions at once: memory, planning, visualization.

That overlap is part of why Caton and Aman think it's worth a closer look. Some researchers use the term cognitive reserve to describe how staying mentally active might help the brain cope with age-related changes. It's a promising idea, not a proven one, and chess isn't a treatment or a cure for anything.

Dr. Kaileigh Byrne at Clemson University, who's been advising Caton and Aman on the project, has pointed out that "there is almost no work specifically on chess" despite its unusual mix of cognitive demands. That gap is part of what got them curious in the first place.

Hippocampus

Memory

Remembering piece positions, past moves, and learned patterns. The hippocampus is among the first regions damaged by Alzheimer's.

Prefrontal Cortex

Planning & Strategy

Thinking multiple moves ahead, evaluating consequences, and managing complex decisions all activate this critical region.

Occipital Lobe

Visualization

Mentally rotating pieces and visualizing future board states engages the brain's visual processing centers intensely.

Cerebellum

Coordination

Even the physical act of playing (moving pieces, managing a clock) engages fine motor coordination and attention.

Working With Clemson
CU
Clemson University Cognitive Psychology · Decision Lab

Caton Tsao and Aman Sreshta brought this project to Dr. Kaileigh Byrne, an associate professor of cognitive psychology at Clemson University, who's been advising them along the way.

The plan is a cross-sectional comparison of long-time chess players and non-players or infrequent players, all aged 65 and older.

The goal is to get a better sense of which brain regions are active during chess play, and whether long-term play lines up with fewer markers of cognitive decline. It's exploratory work, not a clinical trial, so there's no guarantee of what they'll find.

This work started taking shape in 2024 and is still ongoing. Caton and Aman are continuing to work on bringing participants on board.

Why It Matters

The bigger picture.

Alzheimer's affects millions of families. No single student project changes that on its own, but every bit of awareness and curiosity adds up.

6.9 million Americans

Currently living with Alzheimer's disease, a number projected to nearly double by 2060 without new interventions or preventive approaches.

No cure exists yet

There is currently no approved cure for Alzheimer's. Staying mentally active, through activities like chess, is one of many things researchers are looking at, though it's not a substitute for medical care.

Community as a solution

Social connection and mental engagement are both areas researchers are looking at when it comes to brain health as people age. Chess offers a bit of both in one activity.
